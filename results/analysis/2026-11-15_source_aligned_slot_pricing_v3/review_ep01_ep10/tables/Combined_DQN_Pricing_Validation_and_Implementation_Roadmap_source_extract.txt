and Implementation Roadmap
Integrated explanation of passenger acceptance, request-level pricing, driver preferences, DQN learning, CNN demand validation, and Differential Privacy
Prepared as a single working document for implementation and professor review
1. Executive summary and overall objective
The immediate objective is to validate the existing pricing mechanism at the individual passenger-request level before making further changes or attributing results to Differential Privacy. Episode-level acceptance, rejection, assignment and revenue totals are useful, but they do not explain why a particular passenger accepted or rejected a particular offered fare.
The corrected pricing-learning component is a Deep Q-Network (DQN), not a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) and not an actor–critic pricing agent. DQN observes the pricing state, selects an action, and the selected action maps to a pricing factor. That factor determines the offered price. The passenger acceptance mechanism then compares the offered price with that passenger's maximum acceptable price. The resulting interaction contributes to the reward used to train DQN.
The work should proceed in this order: validate request-level passenger pricing; inspect distance and historical-fare behaviour; clarify driver/vehicle price constraints; evaluate DQN learning; validate CNN demand predictions; and only then introduce and evaluate Differential Privacy.
2. End-to-end pricing logic
2.1 DQN action and offered price
At each pricing decision, the DQN agent observes its state and selects an action. The action is mapped to a pricing factor a. The conceptual offered-price equation is:
P_offer (or P_GG′) = P_base × a
The pricing factors discussed in the project include 0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.10 and 1.15. Confirm that the notebook's action-to-factor mapping and the equation above match the actual implementation before treating them as final.
DQN does not directly decide whether a passenger accepts or rejects. DQN selects the pricing action; the passenger decision is made by the acceptance rule below.
2.2 Passenger maximum acceptable price
The meeting explanation expresses the passenger-specific maximum acceptable price as:
P_max,j = P_base × [1 + (1 / ε_j) × ((L_GG′ − L_base) / L_base)]
Symbol
Meaning
P_base
Reference or base fare.
ε_j
Price elasticity / price sensitivity of passenger j, using its absolute value.
L_GG′
Distance-related value for the requested source–destination trip.
L_base
Reference or average trip distance.
P_max,j
Maximum fare passenger j is willing to accept.
If elasticity is already stored as a positive absolute value, use it directly; do not apply an unnecessary second absolute-value operation.
Distance interpretation under the stated formula: if L_GG′ is greater than L_base, P_max is above P_base; if the two are equal, P_max equals P_base; and if L_GG′ is below L_base, P_max is below P_base. A P_max below P_base is therefore not automatically a coding error. Print the exact inputs and intermediate terms from the implementation to verify the result.
2.3 Passenger accept/reject rule
Accept if P_offer ≤ P_max,j
Reject if P_offer > P_max,j
Check whether the code uses < or ≤ at equality and report the same boundary rule in the results. A fare below the base fare can still be rejected if it exceeds the individual passenger's P_max. Therefore, compare P_offer with P_max, not merely with P_base.

4. Interpreting the acceptance rate and assignment outcomes
The meeting refers to an acceptance rate of approximately 29% (about 29% accept and 71% reject) and vehicle availability of approximately 45–90%. The professor is asking why so many passengers reject when vehicles appear to be available.
Keep these measures separate:
Passenger acceptance rate.
Passenger rejection rate.
Accepted requests successfully assigned.
Accepted requests not assigned because of supply or dispatch constraints.
Vehicle availability does not imply that the same percentage of requests can be assigned. Vehicles may be too far away, occupied, ineligible, or unable to satisfy dispatch constraints. The request-level table helps determine whether low acceptance is caused by the fare threshold; assignment analysis then explains what happens after acceptance.

6. Driver / vehicle price preference
Passenger willingness to pay and driver/vehicle price preference are separate constraints. The passenger has a maximum acceptable price P_max,passenger. A driver or vehicle may have a minimum acceptable compensation or price preference P_min,driver.
The meeting discusses a possible lower bound of 0.85 × P_base. For example, if P_base = ₹300, this lower bound is ₹255. However, the recording contains an ambiguity: it first suggests the vehicle preference should not be below P_base, then discusses allowing a lower bound such as 0.85 × P_base. The second guide also notes that the recording does not define a complete driver acceptance equation or an upper bound.
Do not silently finalize either interpretation. Confirm with the professor whether the driver-side minimum is P_base or can go down to 0.85 × P_base, and identify the actual implemented driver constraint. Analyse passenger and driver constraints together: a price acceptable to a passenger may still fail a driver-side constraint.
7. DQN learning and training evaluation
Evaluate DQN behaviour across episodes. The meeting indicates that 25 episodes may reveal an early trend but are too few to claim convergence. A changing reward or action distribution can indicate learning, but does not by itself prove convergence.
Monitor the following DQN-specific outputs:
Episode versus cumulative or average reward.
Episode versus passenger acceptance rate and rejection rate.
Episode versus average offered price.
Frequency of each DQN pricing action / pricing factor.
Q-network loss across training updates.
Stability of action selection and reward over a sufficiently long training run.
Assess stability over a longer run and, where practical, across repeated random seeds or evaluation episodes. Also verify that the reward function reflects the objectives and constraints in the approved research model. The meeting materials do not specify the exact reward equation; retain the equation in the implementation/specification and validate it separately.
8. CNN demand-prediction validation
The professor advises interpreting CNN prediction error relative to the scale of actual demand, rather than judging an absolute RMSE number alone. For the NYC January–March 2026, 30-minute demand-prediction task (48 slots per day), report MAE, RMSE, NRMSE, actual-versus-predicted demand, the evaluation split and the number of observations.
RMSE = √[(1/N) Σ (y_i − ŷ_i)²]
MAE = (1/N) Σ |y_i − ŷ_i|
NRMSE (%) = [RMSE / mean(actual demand)] × 100
Use the mean actual demand across the same grid-slot observations used to calculate RMSE. Do not divide a per-grid-slot RMSE directly by the grand total number of requests across the full dataset; those quantities are at different scales.

9. Differential Privacy: why it comes after baseline validation
First establish a reliable non-private baseline. If the pricing mechanism is already inconsistent before privacy noise is introduced, later changes in acceptance or revenue cannot be attributed confidently to Differential Privacy.
Validate P_base, P_max, offered price and passenger response without Differential Privacy.
Validate DQN reward and learning behaviour in the baseline environment.
Record baseline acceptance, reward, offered price and CNN prediction metrics.
Introduce the low-privacy and high-privacy regimes described in the reference paper.
Test the alpha settings specified by that paper.
Compare privacy-enabled results with the baseline and explain the utility–privacy trade-off.
The meeting materials mention two privacy regimes and alpha but do not define the exact meaning or values of alpha. Obtain the mathematical definition and parameter values from the referenced paper; do not guess them.
